overview_text — فارسی (۵۸۰ کلمه)

کنوانسیون حفاظت از میراث فرهنگی و طبیعی جهان در نوامبر ۱۹۷۲ در هفدهمین اجلاس عمومی یونسکو در پاریس تصویب شد. جرقهٔ آن یک بحران مشخص بود: ساخت سد اسوان در مصر می‌رفت معابد ابوسِمبِل را زیر آب ببرد، و کارزار بین‌المللی نجات آن‌ها برای نخستین‌بار این ایده را جا انداخت که برخی آثار چنان ارزشی دارند که حفاظت از آن‌ها وظیفهٔ همهٔ بشریت است، نه تنها کشور میزبان. مفهوم کلیدی کنوانسیون «ارزش جهانی برجسته» (Outstanding Universal Value) است — اهمیتی چنان استثنایی که از مرزهای ملی فراتر می‌رود. ثبت هر اثر بر پایهٔ ده معیار انجام می‌شود؛ شش معیار نخست (i تا vi) فرهنگی‌اند و چهار معیار پایانی (vii تا x) طبیعی. اثری که هم‌زمان معیارهای هر دو گروه را برآورده کند «مختلط» شناخته می‌شود. اما معیار به‌تنهایی کافی نیست: پروندهٔ هر اثر باید اصالت، یکپارچگی، حریم حفاظتی مشخص و برنامهٔ مدیریت مدون داشته باشد. همین سخت‌گیری است که ثبت جهانی را از یک عنوان تشریفاتی به یک تعهد بلندمدت و تحت پایش تبدیل می‌کند. ایران در سال ۱۹۷۵ به کنوانسیون پیوست و تنها چهار سال بعد، در سومین اجلاس کمیتهٔ میراث جهانی (۱۹۷۹)، سه اثر ایرانی هم‌زمان ثبت شدند: تخت‌جمشید، محوطهٔ چغازنبیل و میدان نقش‌جهان اصفهان. این انتخاب اولیه خودش روایتی است — یک پایتخت آیینی هخامنشی، یک زیگورات ایلامی از هزارهٔ دوم پیش از میلاد، و یک میدان صفوی از قرن هفدهم. یعنی از همان آغاز، آنچه به رسمیت شناخته شد نه یک دورهٔ خاص، بلکه پیوستگی چندهزارسالهٔ یک تمدن بود. امروز — بازبینی اوت ۲۰۲۶ — ایران ۳۰ اثر ثبت‌شده در فهرست میراث جهانی دارد: ۲۸ اثر فرهنگی و ۲ اثر طبیعی. تازه‌ترین ثبت، مجموعهٔ قلعهٔ الموت و استحکامات وابسته در استان قزوین است که در چهل‌وهشتمین اجلاس کمیته (بوسان، ۲۶ ژوئیهٔ ۲۰۲۶) پذیرفته شد — پرونده‌ای سریالی شامل هفت دژ کوهستانی که معماری دفاعی و مهندسی آب‌رسانی اسماعیلیان نزاری را در ارتفاعات البرز مستند می‌کند. دو اثر طبیعی ایران نیز بیابان لوت (۲۰۱۶) و جنگل‌های هیرکانی (۲۰۱۹) هستند؛ نخستی گرم‌ترین سطح ثبت‌شدهٔ کرهٔ زمین و دومی بازماندهٔ جنگل‌های پهن‌برگی که در دوران سوم زمین‌شناسی بخش بزرگی از نیم‌کرهٔ شمالی را می‌پوشاند. آنچه این مجموعه دربارهٔ ایران می‌گوید، فراتر از شمار آن است. در میان این سی اثر، هم شهر سوختهٔ سیستان با شهرنشینی هزارهٔ سوم پیش از میلاد هست، هم قنات ایرانی که اصلاً بنا نیست بلکه یک فناوری زندهٔ آب است و کشاورزی را در فلاتی خشک ممکن کرد؛ هم باغ ایرانی که تصویری از بهشت زمینی ساخت و طراحی باغ را از اندلس تا هند گورکانی شکل داد، هم مجموعهٔ بازار تاریخی تبریز که قرن‌ها گرهگاه تجاری جاده‌های ابریشم بود. از معماری خشتی یزد تا مجموعه‌های رهبانی ارمنیان در شمال‌غرب و راه‌آهن سراسری ایران، این فهرست در برابر هر روایت واحدی دربارهٔ «ایران چیست» مقاومت می‌کند. از نظر عملی، این آثار در بیش از بیست استان پراکنده‌اند و بازدید از همهٔ آن‌ها در یک سفر شدنی نیست. کارآمدترین مسیر، محور مرکزی تهران–کاشان–اصفهان–یزد–شیراز است که به‌تنهایی حدود یک‌سوم فهرست را پوشش می‌دهد. فروردین و اردیبهشت و نیز مهر و آبان برای فلات مرکزی و آثار کویری مناسب‌ترند؛ جنگل‌های هیرکانی و کلیساهای شمال‌غرب در تابستان دلپذیرترند. ایران افزون بر این، بیش از پنجاه اثر در فهرست موقت خود دارد — قرار گرفتن در این فهرست پیش‌شرط رسمی هر نامزدی آینده است.

overview_text — English (560 words)

The Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage was adopted at UNESCO's General Conference in Paris in November 1972. Its origin was a crisis: Egypt's Aswan High Dam threatened to submerge the temples of Abu Simbel, and the international rescue campaign that followed established a then-novel principle — that certain places matter so much that safeguarding them is a duty shared by all humanity, not only by the state that happens to contain them. The Convention's organising idea is Outstanding Universal Value: significance so exceptional that it transcends national boundaries. Inscription rests on ten criteria. The first six (i–vi) are cultural; the last four (vii–x) are natural. A property meeting criteria from both groups is classified as mixed. Meeting a criterion is not sufficient on its own: a nomination must also demonstrate authenticity, integrity, defined buffer zones and a documented management plan. That rigour is what separates World Heritage status from an honorary title — it is a long-term, monitored commitment. Iran ratified the Convention in 1975 and, just four years later at the third session of the World Heritage Committee in 1979, saw three properties inscribed simultaneously: Persepolis, the ziggurat complex of Tchogha Zanbil, and Meidan Emam in Isfahan. That opening selection is itself revealing — an Achaemenid ceremonial capital, an Elamite ziggurat from the second millennium BCE, and a seventeenth-century Safavid square. From the outset, what was recognised was not one era but the continuity of a civilisation across several thousand years. As of August 2026, Iran has 30 inscribed World Heritage properties: 28 cultural and 2 natural. The most recent addition is the serial property of Alamut Castle and its associated fortifications in Qazvin Province, inscribed at the Committee's 48th session in Busan on 26 July 2026. It comprises seven mountain strongholds that together document the defensive architecture and water-supply engineering of the Nizari Ismaili state in the Alborz range. Iran's two natural properties are the Lut Desert (2016), where the highest land-surface temperature on Earth has been recorded, and the Hyrcanian Forests (2019), a surviving remnant of the broadleaf forests that covered much of the northern temperate zone during the Tertiary period. What the collection reveals about Iran matters more than its size. It includes Shahr-i Sokhta, a Bronze Age urban settlement in Sistan dating to the third millennium BCE, and the Persian Qanat — not a monument at all but a living water technology that made agriculture possible across an arid plateau. It includes the Persian Garden, which codified an idea of paradise that shaped garden design from Andalusia to Mughal India, and the Tabriz Historic Bazaar Complex, a commercial nerve centre of the Silk Roads for centuries. From the adobe architecture of Yazd to the Armenian monastic ensembles of the northwest and the Trans-Iranian Railway, the list resists any single narrative about what Iran is. Practically, these sites are distributed across more than twenty provinces and cannot be covered in one trip. The most efficient route is the central corridor — Tehran, Kashan, Isfahan, Yazd, Shiraz — which alone takes in roughly a third of the list. April–May and October–November suit the central plateau and desert sites; the Hyrcanian forests and northwestern churches are most rewarding in summer. Iran additionally maintains a Tentative List of more than fifty properties, placement on which is the formal prerequisite for any future nomination.

اعتُمدت اتفاقية حماية التراث الثقافي والطبيعي العالمي في المؤتمر العام لليونسكو بباريس في تشرين الثاني/نوفمبر ١٩٧٢. وكان الدافع إليها أزمة محددة: إذ هدّد السد العالي في أسوان بإغراق معبدَي أبو سمبل، فجاءت الحملة الدولية لإنقاذهما لترسّخ مبدأً جديداً حينها، وهو أنّ بعض المواقع تبلغ من الأهمية حدّاً يجعل صونها واجباً على البشرية جمعاء لا على الدولة المضيفة وحدها. والمفهوم المحوري في الاتفاقية هو «القيمة العالمية الاستثنائية». يقوم التسجيل على عشرة معايير: الستة الأولى (i–vi) ثقافية، والأربعة الأخيرة (vii–x) طبيعية، ويوصف الموقع المستوفي لمعايير من المجموعتين بأنه «مختلط». غير أنّ استيفاء المعيار وحده لا يكفي؛ إذ يجب أن يبرهن الملف على الأصالة والسلامة، وأن يحدّد المنطقة العازلة، وأن يقدّم خطة إدارة موثقة. هذه الصرامة هي ما يحوّل التسجيل العالمي من لقب تشريفي إلى التزام طويل الأمد خاضع للمتابعة. انضمت إيران إلى الاتفاقية عام ١٩٧٥، وبعد أربع سنوات فقط سُجّلت ثلاثة مواقع إيرانية دفعةً واحدة في الدورة الثالثة للجنة التراث العالمي عام ١٩٧٩: برسبوليس (تخت جمشيد)، ومجمّع زقّورة تشغازنبيل، وميدان نقش جهان في أصفهان. وفي هذا الاختيار الأول دلالة بحد ذاته: عاصمة أخمينية طقسية، وزقّورة عيلامية من الألف الثاني قبل الميلاد، وميدان صفوي من القرن السابع عشر. أي أنّ ما اعتُرف به منذ البداية لم يكن حقبة بعينها، بل استمرارية حضارة امتدت آلاف السنين. وحتى آب/أغسطس ٢٠٢٦، تملك إيران ثلاثين موقعاً مسجّلاً في قائمة التراث العالمي: ثمانية وعشرون ثقافياً واثنان طبيعيان. وأحدثها قلعة ألموت والتحصينات المرتبطة بها في محافظة قزوين، التي سُجّلت في الدورة الثامنة والأربعين للجنة في بوسان بتاريخ ٢٦ تموز/يوليو ٢٠٢٦، وهي ملف تسلسلي يضم سبع قلاع جبلية توثّق العمارة الدفاعية وهندسة إمداد المياه لدى الإسماعيليين النزاريين في جبال ألبرز. أما الموقعان الطبيعيان فهما صحراء لوت (٢٠١٦)، صاحبة أعلى درجة حرارة سطحية سُجّلت على الأرض، والغابات الهيركانية (٢٠١٩)، وهي بقايا غابات عريضة الأوراق تعود إلى الحقبة الثالثة. وما تكشفه هذه المجموعة عن إيران أهم من عددها. فهي تضم مدينة شهر سوخته في سيستان من الألف الثالث قبل الميلاد، وتضم «القنوات الفارسية» التي ليست أثراً جامداً بل تقنية مائية حيّة أتاحت الزراعة في هضبة قاحلة. وتضم الحديقة الفارسية التي صاغت تصوراً للجنة الأرضية أثّر في تصميم الحدائق من الأندلس إلى الهند المغولية، وسوق تبريز التاريخي الذي كان لقرونٍ عقدةً تجارية على طرق الحرير. ومن عمارة يزد الطينية إلى الأديرة الأرمنية في الشمال الغربي وسكة حديد إيران العابرة، تأبى القائمة أن تُختزل في رواية واحدة. ومن الناحية العملية، تتوزع هذه المواقع على أكثر من عشرين محافظة، ولا يمكن زيارتها جميعاً في رحلة واحدة. وأكثر المسارات كفاءةً هو المحور الأوسط: طهران – كاشان – أصفهان – يزد – شيراز، وهو وحده يغطي نحو ثلث القائمة. ويُنصح بشهري نيسان وأيار وتشرين الأول والثاني لزيارة الهضبة الوسطى والمواقع الصحراوية، بينما تكون الغابات الهيركانية وكنائس الشمال الغربي أجمل في الصيف. كما تحتفظ إيران بقائمة مؤقتة تضم أكثر من خمسين موقعاً، والإدراج فيها شرط رسمي مسبق لأي ترشيح مستقبلي.

overview_text — English (540 words)

Until the early twenty-first century, the international heritage system had a significant blind spot: whatever could not be touched had no place in it. A thousand-year-old building could be inscribed and protected, while the ritual performed inside it for the same thousand years had no safeguarding mechanism at all. The Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, adopted in 2003 and in force from 2006, was written to close precisely that gap. The Convention covers five domains: oral traditions and expressions, including language as a vehicle of heritage; performing arts; social practices, rituals and festive events; knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe; and traditional craftsmanship. Its philosophical break with the World Heritage Convention lies in the admission criterion. There is no test of "outstanding value" here, because no culture is held to rank above another. What matters is that the heritage is living, that the community bearing it recognises it as part of its identity, and that it is transmitted and recreated across generations. For this reason the free, prior and informed consent of the bearer community is a non-negotiable requirement of every file. Three separate mechanisms exist: the Representative List, which illustrates the diversity of human heritage; the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding, for elements whose survival is at risk; and the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, which highlights programmes others can learn from. Iran ratified the Convention in 2006, and its first inscription came in 2009: Nowruz. That file was multinational from the outset and today is inscribed with the participation of twelve states — a reminder that intangible heritage, unlike a monument, does not respect political borders but flows across a cultural region stretching from the Balkans to Central Asia. As of August 2026, Iran has 27 inscribed elements: 24 on the Representative List, 2 on the Urgent Safeguarding List, and 1 in the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices. The most recent addition is the art of mirror-work (āyene-kāri) in Iranian architecture, inscribed at the Committee's twentieth session in New Delhi in December 2025. The two elements on the Urgent Safeguarding List were both inscribed in 2011, and their warning is serious. Naqqāli, Iranian dramatic storytelling, has seen its performer base contract sharply with the decline of the traditional coffee house that once housed it. The skills of building and sailing Iranian Lenj boats in the Persian Gulf are eroding as engines and fibreglass hulls displace a body of knowledge that combined shipwrightry with celestial navigation. Both are reminders that inscription is not a conclusion but the declaration of an emergency. A substantial share of Iran's files are multinational — from Nowruz and Mehregān (shared with Tajikistan) to Sadeh and the art of crafting and playing the Rubab. These joint nominations trace a cultural sphere considerably wider than any modern border. For travellers, intangible heritage cannot be visited the way a monument can; it has to be coincided with. Nowruz falls in late March, Sadeh in late January or early February, and Mehregān in early October. Living craft workshops, however — Kashan's carpet weavers, Isfahan's tilework and mirror-work ateliers — are open year-round and offer the most direct point of contact.

UNESCO Global Geopark is the youngest of the organisation's well-known designations. Although the Global Geoparks Network had operated since 2004, it was only in November 2015 that UNESCO's General Conference adopted the International Geoscience and Geoparks Programme, giving the label formal standing within the organisation. What distinguishes a geopark from a national park or protected area is philosophical. A geopark is not an enclosure. It is a unified territory holding geological heritage of international significance, managed on three pillars: conservation of geological features, education in the earth sciences, and sustainable development of the local community. The people living in the territory are not an obstacle to the designation but its principal actors, expected to benefit directly from geotourism. This three-pillar framework makes Geopark one of the few UNESCO labels with local economic development written into its definition. The designation is also conditional and periodically re-examined. Every geopark undergoes a full revalidation every four years and receives either a green card or a yellow card. A geopark that fails to address the deficiencies behind a yellow card within two years loses its status. Geopark is, in the strict sense, a condition rather than a permanent honour. Iran holds three UNESCO Global Geoparks. The first is Qeshm Island Geopark in Hormozgan Province, which received global status in 2017 and was at that time the only geopark in the Middle East. Qeshm's trajectory is instructive: it first joined the network in 2006 but was removed in 2012 over shortcomings in management and conservation, returning only after its governance structure had been overhauled. That single case demonstrates how seriously the revalidation process is applied. The other two were both admitted in 2023. Aras Geopark, in East Azerbaijan Province around Jolfa, contains an exceptionally legible Permian–Triassic boundary — the stratigraphic record of the most severe mass extinction in Earth's history — and has for decades been a field site for international geological research. Tabas Geopark in South Khorasan is the largest geopark in West Asia and assembles an unusually wide range of features, from banded rock exposures and crystal-lined canyons to Jurassic plant fossils and open desert landscapes. Taken together, the three tell Iran's geological story: a country seated on the collision of several tectonic plates, and therefore able to hold salt domes and coral islands, extinction boundaries and fossil-bearing deserts within a comparatively small geographic compass. It is a place where deep time can be traversed in a matter of hours. For visiting, Qeshm is best from November through March; Persian Gulf summers are extremely hot and humid. Aras is at its finest in May, June and October. Tabas, like any desert region, is comfortable in autumn, winter and early spring, with summer daytime temperatures exceeding fifty degrees Celsius. All three geoparks maintain visitor information centres and waymarked geotourism trails.

The Man and the Biosphere Programme, universally abbreviated to MAB, was launched in 1971 and grew out of a simple but fundamental question: does protecting nature necessarily mean removing people from it? MAB's answer was no. Instead of sealed enclosures, the programme proposed territories in which conservation, research and human life could coexist. The instrument for realising that idea is a three-zone model that every biosphere reserve must implement. The core area is under full legal protection and admits only low-impact research. The buffer zone surrounds the core and hosts education, ecotourism and field study. The transition area is the outermost layer, where people live and farm and where sustainable land-use practices are tested. Together, the three zones make a biosphere reserve something close to a living laboratory for sustainable development. An important legal point: a biosphere reserve does not limit national sovereignty and introduces no new law of its own. It is an international recognition layered on top of existing national legislation. The designation is therefore an instrument of scientific cooperation and visibility rather than of enforcement. Iran was among the programme's pioneers in Asia. In 1976, only five years after MAB began, nine Iranian reserves joined the world network simultaneously: Golestan, Miankaleh, Lake Urmia, Arasbaran, Arjan and Parishan, Hara, Geno, Kavir and Touran. A group admission on that scale was unprecedented at the time and reflected an early decision to treat Iran's biodiversity as a network rather than a set of isolated points. After a long interval, a second wave began in the 2010s: Dena (2010) in the central Zagros, Tang-e Sayad and Sabzkuh (2015) in Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, Hamoun (2016) in Sistan, and Kopet Dagh (2018) in Khorasan. These brought Iran's total to thirteen biosphere reserves. What the thirteen collectively map is Iran's ecological range — a country containing Caspian temperate forest, central desert, internationally significant wetland, marine mangrove and Zagros highland. Several of them also document crisis: Lake Urmia and the Hamoun wetlands have both undergone severe desiccation in recent decades, making them living indicators of the region's water and climate condition. For visitors, the core area of any reserve normally requires a permit from the provincial Department of Environment, while buffer and transition zones are generally open and hold most of the ecotourism facilities. Golestan and Arasbaran are at their finest in May–June and October–November; Hara and Geno in the south during the cooler half of the year; Kavir and Touran in autumn and early spring. Contacting the provincial Department of Environment in advance — both for permits and for a local guide — is always advisable.

The UNESCO Creative Cities Network was founded in 2004 and, unlike most of the organisation's programmes, is not about protecting the past but investing in the future. Its central question is whether culture and creativity can serve as engines of sustainable urban development. A city joining the network commits to placing its cultural industries at the centre of its development planning and to sharing its experience with member cities worldwide. The network covers seven fields: crafts and folk art, gastronomy, literature, design, film, music, and media arts. In 2025 UNESCO added a new category, architecture, for cities whose architectural heritage and contemporary practice are central to their identity. What distinguishes this designation is that it carries obligations. Member cities must submit a membership report every four years demonstrating what they have done — supporting local artists and artisans, hosting international events, collaborating with cities in the same field. It is an active membership rather than a static honour. Iran has six Creative Cities in the network. The first two were admitted in 2015: Isfahan for crafts and folk art, and Rasht for gastronomy. Isfahan, with its workshops in metal engraving, enamel, khatam marquetry and turquoise inlay, was an obvious choice. Rasht made a subtler point — Gilani cooking, with its range of herbs, pickles and rice dishes, is among the most distinctive culinary traditions in the region. Two more joined in 2019: Sanandaj for music, reflecting its place in Kurdish musical tradition and instruments such as the daf and tanbur; and Bandar Abbas for crafts, drawing on the needlework and weaving traditions of the southern coast. Kermanshah was then admitted as a city of gastronomy in 2021. The most recent member is Kashan, selected in 2025 in the newly created architecture category and thus among the world's first Cities of Architecture — international recognition, in a new frame, of its historic courtyard houses, wind towers and adobe urban fabric. Together the six make a point about geography: creativity in Iran is not concentrated. From the Persian Gulf coast to the Kurdish Zagros, and from the Caspian shore to the edge of the central desert, each region sustains its own living creative tradition. Unlike monuments, a Creative City is experienced in bazaars, workshops and restaurants. In Isfahan and Kashan, craft ateliers and historic houses are open year-round. overview_text — English (440 words)

National Natural Monument is the only designation among the six covered here that originates in Iran's own legal system rather than an international convention. Its statutory basis is Article 3 of the Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act of 1974, which established four categories of managed area: national park, national natural monument, wildlife refuge, and protected area. Each designation is enacted by resolution of the High Council for Environmental Protection. What separates a national natural monument from the other three categories is scale and logic. A national park or protected area is normally a large territory with an integrated ecosystem. A national natural monument focuses instead on a singular phenomenon — an ancient tree, a waterfall, a summit, a rare plant habitat, a cave, or an exceptional geological structure. The criterion is uniqueness rather than extent, which is why some of Iran's national natural monuments cover only a few hectares, and in certain cases amount to the protected perimeter of a single tree. The first designation came in 2002: Mount Damavand, Iran's highest peak and the highest volcano in Asia, which also occupies a distinctive place in Persian myth and literature. The following year, 2003, brought the Cypress of Abarkuh in Yazd Province — a tree estimated to be more than four thousand years old and counted among the oldest living organisms on Earth. Today more than forty national natural monuments are registered across upwards of twenty provinces, covering in total more than 41,000 hectares. Among them are the Fritillaria plain of Koohrang in Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari, which turns crimson for a few weeks each spring; the habitat of the Chelcheragh lily in Gilan, the only natural habitat of this rare flower anywhere in the world; and Shevi Waterfall in Khuzestan. One distinction should be made clear. "National natural monument" falls under the Department of Environment, whereas "national natural heritage" is a separate designation administered by the Ministry of Cultural Heritage, Tourism and Handicrafts. The two are regularly conflated in the press; this site covers only the former. Most of these sites lie in mountainous and rural areas, and reaching them generally requires a suitable vehicle and sometimes a local guide. Timing is critical for several: the Fritillaria plain flowers for only a few weeks in April and May, and the Chelcheragh lily has a similarly short window in late spring. Contacting the provincial Department of Environment before travelling — for access conditions and any permit requirement — is recommended.

فارسیتفاوت اثر طبیعی ملی با میراث طبیعی ملی چیست؟اثر طبیعی ملی زیر نظر سازمان حفاظت محیط زیست ثبت می‌شود، اما میراث طبیعی ملی عنوانی جداست که وزارت میراث فرهنگی، گردشگری و صنایع دستی آن را ثبت می‌کند. این دو در رسانه‌ها اغلب اشتباه گرفته می‌شوند.
فارسیبرای بازدید چه نکاتی مهم است؟بیشتر این آثار در مناطق کوهستانی و روستایی‌اند و خودروی مناسب لازم دارند. برای برخی زمان‌بندی حیاتی است؛ مثلاً دشت لاله‌های واژگون تنها چند هفته در اردیبهشت گل می‌دهد. تماس پیشاپیش با ادارهٔ محیط زیست استان توصیه می‌شود.
EnglishWhat is a national natural monument?One of four managed-area categories under Iran's Department of Environment, established by Article 3 of the 1974 Environmental Protection Act. Unlike a national park, it protects a singular phenomenon rather than a broad ecosystem.
EnglishWhich was Iran's first national natural monument?Mount Damavand, designated in 2002. The Cypress of Abarkuh in Yazd followed the next year.
EnglishHow does it differ from national natural heritage?National natural monuments are registered by the Department of Environment, while national natural heritage is a separate designation under the Ministry of Cultural Heritage, Tourism and Handicrafts. The two are frequently confused in the press.
EnglishWhat should visitors know?Most sites are mountainous or rural and require a suitable vehicle. Timing matters for some — the Fritillaria plain flowers for only a few weeks in April and May. Contact the provincial Department of Environment in advance.
